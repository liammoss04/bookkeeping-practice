# Small-Business Bookkeeping \- Practice Set (Q1 2026\)

A hands-on bookkeeping project: recording a full quarter of trading for a small business using double-entry bookkeeping, then producing a trial balance, a profit & loss account, a balance sheet and a bank reconciliation.

> Note: this uses a **practice (sample) set of transactions** for a fictional cafe, "The Daily Grind Cafe", not a real client. It was built to demonstrate the mechanics of double-entry bookkeeping end to end.

---

## Contents

- `Bookkeeping_Project_Cafe.xlsx` \- the full workbook (transactions, journal, trial balance, financial statements and bank reconciliation)  
- `README.md` \- this summary

## What this demonstrates

- Recording transactions by **double entry** (equal debits and credits)  
- Preparing a **trial balance** and proving it balances  
- Producing a **profit & loss account** and a **balance sheet** from the ledgers  
- Performing a **bank reconciliation**, including identifying items not yet in the books

## Method

A fictional cafe's first quarter (January to March 2026\) is given as 27 business transactions. Each was posted to a general journal by double entry; the trial balance, financial statements and the adjusted side of the bank reconciliation are then driven by formulas, so the workbook re-checks itself and flags whether each statement balances.

## Key figures (quarter to 31 Mar 2026\)

| Measure | Value |
| :---- | :---- |
| Sales | £14,500 |
| Net profit | £1,825 |
| Equipment (at cost) | £5,800 |
| Bank balance at quarter end | £12,725 |
| Bank loan outstanding | £5,500 |
| Net assets / owner's equity | £13,025 |

---

## What the numbers show

> 

- The cafe was profitable in its very first quarter (£1,825), which is a good sign given it also spent £5,800 on equipment up front.  
- It ended the quarter with a healthy cash position (£12,725 in the bank) and only modest debt (£5,500 loan outstanding after an early repayment), so it is not over-reliant on borrowing.  
- The bank reconciliation identified two differences between the cashbook and the bank statement: a £2,900 deposit in transit (cash banked on 31 March that had not yet cleared) and a £25 bank charge that the bank knew about but had not yet been recorded in the books.  
- Profit and Cash are not the same thing here; The business made £1,825 profit, but its bank balance rose far more than that over the quarter, because the owner’s £12,000 capital and the £6,000 loan are cash inflows that aren’t income and the £5,800 of equipment is capitalised on the balance sheet rather than expensed. 

>   
> 

## Simplifications (deliberate, for clarity)

- VAT is ignored.  
- Loan interest is ignored (only the principal repayment is recorded).  
- Stock is expensed as "Purchases" when bought, rather than carried as inventory.
